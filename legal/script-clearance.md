# Script Clearance: The Report, the Chain, and the Gate

**Not legal advice.** What follows is the script-clearance system as documented in the clearance literature researched for this guide (research cycle 81, Identity r78, Film script-clearance rules 1358 to 1367, from six sources fetched in full: an entertainment law firm's clearance guidelines, WIPO's rights-clearance guide for independent filmmakers, an entertainment lawyer's account, a law-office clearance FAQ, an insurer's E&O page, and a film-insurance coverage note). It describes what the documents are, when each is required, and how a production assembles them. It invents no legal claims beyond those sources; where the research is silent, this chapter says so. Clearance for a real production belongs with a clearance researcher and an entertainment attorney.

The guide's rights chapter covers disclosure, likeness, and music. This chapter covers the layer underneath all of them: **what may lawfully appear in the work at all**, every name, place, business, object, and the title itself, researched against the real world before and during production, and proved in a file that an insurer and a distributor will both demand to see.

## The system in one pass

1. The **final shooting script** is researched by a professional clearance researcher; the result is the **clearance report** (rule 1358).
2. The writer supplies an **annotation guide** labelling every character, place, and event fictional, real, or composite (rule 1359).
3. Names, businesses, and addresses that coincide with real, identifiable people or entities are changed or released; only **permitted telephone and plate numbers** ever appear (rule 1360).
4. Ownership is proved as an unbroken **chain of title**, verified by a **copyright report** (rule 1361).
5. The film's own title is researched in a **title report** before final selection (rule 1362).
6. Written **releases** cover depicted persons, performers, extras, crew, and distinctive locations (rule 1363).
7. Featured **props, products, artwork, and photographs** are cleared one by one, under an abundance of caution (rule 1364).
8. The report is a **living document**, updated whenever new material enters, at every stage (rule 1365).
9. Every flagged item closes with a **recorded decision**, license, substitute, change the use, or expressly assume the risk (rule 1366).
10. Before exhibition, the **finished film is checked against the file**, trailers get their own analysis, and the completed file is the **gate**: no completed clearance, no E&O cover and no distribution (rule 1367).

The working tool built on these ten rules, a clearance file you can run an AI-film production through, item by item, with the gate verdict computed from your own entries, is the guide's [Script clearance desk](../clearance.html).

## The clearance report (rule 1358)

**What it is.** The system's founding document, commissioned rather than improvised: a professional clearance research company researches the final shooting script. Every story character name and place is checked against how common that name, street address, and business name actually is in the general population, so a fictional person does not accidentally coincide with a real one who could claim to have been portrayed. The same report identifies the proper contact from whom to obtain permission for any product, copyright, or trademark the script references. WIPO's guide states the same instrument from the producer's side: the clearance report, prepared by a legal expert, is the key document structuring the whole process, it identifies the objects and materials in the project that carry infringement risk, and it can carry title reports and clearance/release submission logs as attached parts.

**When it is required.** Before production, the report is filed on the final shooting script, and (rule 1365) it does not stop there.

**How a production assembles it.** Lock the shooting script; send it, with the annotation guide below, to the clearance researcher; file the returned report as the production's master clearance record, with the title report and release logs attached.

## The annotation guide (rule 1359)

**What it is.** A document the writer supplies, identifying every character, place, and event in the script and stating whether each is purely fictional, real, or a composite of real and fictitious material, because that label is what determines which further releases are needed. Two companion records pin the work's origins down: the stimulus and sequence by which an "original" unpublished work was created are documented, and if a similar script by a different writer was ever submitted to the same company, the wholly independent path of the later script is documented and evaluated, so an infringement claim can be answered from the file. For true-life stories the discipline extends to sources: whether the writer relied on independent, primary sources (contemporaneous reports, court transcripts, public records) or on secondary, copyrighted works, the latter ordinarily requiring a release. A law-office FAQ states the same starting point in plain form: the review begins from an annotated script describing every character, place, and event, real or fictitious, and everything based on underlying property.

**When it is required.** At the start, it is the clearance review's input, not its output.

**How a production assembles it.** The writer annotates the script element by element (fictional / real / composite), documents the work's origins and any similar submission's independent path, and records true-life sources as primary or secondary.

## Researched names and permitted numbers (rule 1360)

**What it is.** The day-to-day substance of the research is names. Every single character's name in the script is checked, against the possibility that a real person with that exact name believes they are being portrayed without permission; all proposed signage for stores, institutions, and other locations is researched against copyright and trademark restrictions; and only certain phone and license plate numbers may be used at all, the permitted-number discipline that keeps a fictional number from ringing a real subscriber. The insurer's page states the failure mode the research prevents as a named coverage class: claims arising from character names, business names, addresses, or other identifying details in the screenplay that match real-world individuals or entities, which is why a full script clearance search precedes production.

**The rule.** A fictional name, business, or address that coincides with a real, identifiable person or entity is changed or released before use, coincidence is a defect, not a defence, and only permitted telephone and plate numbers ever appear.

**For AI-film productions.** Generated signage, storefronts, documents, and plates are exactly where unresearched names and numbers slip in, a model will happily render a plausible business name that belongs to someone. The research is silent on AI-generated signage as a special case; the rule above is stated for scripts, and this guide applies it to anything the finished film shows, because rule 1367's final check covers what the script never contained. That application is the guide's practice, labelled as such, not a claim from the research.

## Chain of title and the copyright report (rule 1361)

**What it is.** Under the script sits the property record. The producer documents the origin of the work and each transaction that transferred rights in it up to the producer, the chain of title, testing whether the writer's engagement was a bona fide work for hire and, if not, whether an effective option or transfer exists; potential rights-holders are identified (including contributors under earlier, expired options whose rewrite contributions may outlive them), anyone who contributed content without giving a release is found, and quitclaims are obtained from anyone holding an encumbrance on a clean chain. A separate copyright report verifies the chain, domestic and foreign copyrights and renewal rights, and is always required by distributors; a party acquiring a completed film performs the same review again on the underlying property. WIPO states the stakes in one line of structure: the chain of title is the unbroken record of ownership, and it is a condition for the film's circulation in the market, expressly asserted to distributors and sales agents.

**When it is required.** From acquisition onward, and again at delivery, when the distributor and the acquirer re-run the review.

**How a production assembles it.** File the origin document and every transfer in order; test each work-for-hire or option; find unreleased contributors; quitclaim encumbrances; commission the copyright report covering domestic, foreign, and renewal rights. The guide's rights chapter makes the same point from the AI side: an AI track or image produces a thin rights file, and the paperwork is where that thinness is caught.

## The title report (rule 1362)

**What it is.** The film's own name goes through the same discipline as its characters'. A title report is obtained prior to final title selection: it sets out prior uses of the same or similar titles, and if there is a conflict, the title is changed; for an additional fee the reporting company adds a legal opinion on whether the title is clear. Distributors require the report, and the E&O application file shows why: the application itself includes a title report completed by a title search company, expressly to ensure the production's title is not too similar to an existing production. At the end of the process the title is chosen and again submitted for research, against the real frequency of same-and-similar titles already released.

**The rule.** A work's title is researched before final selection; the report lists prior same and similar uses, a conflict changes the title, and the report is filed for the distributors and insurers who require it.

## Release coverage (rule 1363)

**What it is.** What the report flags is closed with paper. Written releases from living persons recognizably depicted, a class that expressly includes thinly disguised versions and persons identifiable through other characters or the factual, historical, or geographic setting, and, for deceased persons, from their personal representatives or heirs; insurers insist on these unless the production demonstrates in writing why a release is unnecessary. Performers and recognizable extras release not just image and name but the production's rights to edit, delete, juxtapose, and fictionalize; crew contracts carry likeness releases too, against the day a crew member is captured on camera or in behind-the-scenes footage. Distinctive locations, buildings, businesses, and personal property filmed get written releases; non-distinctive background use usually does not. A law-office FAQ's three-category form, property, people, locations, matches the same coverage, with releases from all persons appearing, crew included.

**The rule.** Written releases cover every recognizably depicted person, thinly disguised and deceased included, the latter via representatives or heirs, every performer, recognizable extra and crew member, and every distinctive location.

## The featured-object caution (rule 1364)

**What it is.** Props, set dressing, and wardrobe bearing distinctive logos are cleared, under a stated abundance of caution, because a prop's prominence in the final cut cannot be anticipated, and items from prop houses or prior films are never assumed already cleared. Artwork demands the same caution in full, even unfocused work, with the artist, their death date, first publication, and copyright holder all established; photographs and posters need releases from photographer, subject, and holder alike.

**The rule.** Props, products, artwork, and photographs are cleared one by one under an abundance of caution, prominence in the final form is unknowable in advance, and supplied or previously used items are never assumed already cleared.

**For AI-film productions.** A generated frame is full of featured objects nobody placed there deliberately: artwork on a wall, a poster, a product on a shelf. The research addresses props and artwork in shot productions; it is silent on generated set dressing as a category. The guide's practice, clear what the finished film prominently shows, item by item, at the final check, follows from rules 1364 and 1367 read together, and is labelled as practice, not as a researched claim.

## The living report (rule 1365)

**What it is.** Clearance does not end at the script. The report can and should be updated as more materials are included, in the script, the schedule, the storyboard, the actual shooting, and even postproduction, extending to shot-by-shot analysis. Clearance starts as early as possible, with preproduction as the latest responsible start, because late-found rights are the expensive kind. Filmmaker and attorney monitor the production at all stages, from inception through final cut.

**The rule.** The clearance report is updated whenever new material enters, script versions, boards, schedules, the shoot, post-production, and clearance monitoring runs at all stages, not as a single pass at the start.

## The recorded decision (rule 1366)

**What it is.** Each flagged item is closed by a decision, taken with the legal expert: license the content, substitute a different element, use it in a different way, or keep the use as it is without licensing, an express assumption of risk, recorded as such.

**The rule.** Every flagged item is closed by a recorded decision, license it, substitute it, change the way it is used, or expressly assume the risk, and an undecided flagged item blocks the element it flags. The clearance desk's gate verdict is this rule, computed: one open flag holds the gate.

## The final check and the E&O / distribution gate (rule 1367)

**What it is.** Before any public exhibition the attorney previews the finished film to assure the procedures were followed, because the film contains things no script ever listed: the lawyer's account describes the rough version reviewed precisely for what slipped in, a protected mark caught in the background of an outdoor shot. Advertising is not inherited clearance: trailers and commercial tie-ins get a separate analysis that licenses extend to publicity use, and bonus content is cleared like the primary production. Known rights problems arising after application must still be disclosed to the insurer. The completed file is what the gates check: without completed clearance procedures there is no E&O policy, and without an E&O policy the distributors and platforms will not license or acquire the film at all. E&O itself is content-liability cover, defamation, copyright infringement, invasion of privacy, required by distributors and platforms before licensing or acquisition, and script-clearance failures are one of its named claim classes.

**The E&O application file**, as the coverage note itemises it: the producer's biography, the script, synopsis, and production copy for review, the clearance reports, and a title report completed by a title search company. The insurer's 12-point clearance checklist likewise opens with chain of title and full-cast script clearance, and carries ongoing disclosure and bonus-content clearance as standing items.

**The rule.** Before exhibition the finished work is reviewed against the clearance file for what the script never contained, trailers and publicity receive their own clearance analysis, and the completed file is the gate, no completed clearance, no cover and no distribution.

## What the research does not say

Honesty about the edges, in the guide's usual form:

- The six sources describe the system for conventionally produced films. Their application to AI-generated material in this chapter is marked, each time, as the guide's practice derived from the rules, the sources do not address generative pipelines, model outputs, or synthetic performers.
- The sources name no prices, turnaround times, or named clearance companies' rates for any document above, and this chapter quotes none.
- The sources are US-practice documents (plus WIPO's international guide). Jurisdiction-specific requirements beyond what is stated here are outside the research; the chapter does not generalise them.
- Nothing here is legal advice, and no checklist in this guide substitutes for a clearance researcher and an entertainment attorney on a real production.
