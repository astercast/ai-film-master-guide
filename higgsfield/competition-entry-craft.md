# Competition-entry craft

Judged AI-film competitions are a production teacher, not a lottery ticket. This chapter is about what their constraints and judging criteria teach you about making professional work, whether or not you ever enter one.

## Why competitions are worth studying

A competition hands you three things most productions never get:

1. **Hard constraints**, runtime limits, delivery specs, content rules. Constraints are a forcing function for craft: every decision the rules make for you is a decision you can't get wrong.
2. **A published quality bar**, judging criteria tell you exactly what experienced eyes score: directing, craft, sound, originality. That's a checklist you can aim any film at.
3. **A body of judged work**, winning entries are public. Shot-by-shot teardowns of what placed are the cheapest film school available (see `case-studies/competition-teardowns.md`).

## The constraint set that shapes production

Real competition rulebooks converge on the same production discipline. Treat each as a standing habit:

- **Runtime discipline.** Competitions cap entries at a few minutes. The craft lesson: a 3-minute film that lands beats a 10-minute film that drifts. Budget your runtime like your credits, every shot must earn its seconds.
- **Delivery specs are real.** Watermarks, packshots, aspect ratios, masters. The craft lesson: professional delivery is a package, not a file. Build your delivery checklist before you lock picture (see `skills/delivery/`).
- **Rights hygiene.** Competition rules typically require AI-generated music/voice/sound with rights held by the creator. The craft lesson: clear every asset's rights at generation time, not at delivery. Keep the receipts in your asset registry.
- **Likeness safety.** "No real person's face or voice" is a common rule, not even your own. The craft lesson: never build a film on likeness you can't clear. Synthetic characters and Soul IDs are the safe default.
- **Small teams, async.** Winning entries are routinely made by 2 to 4 people across time zones. The craft lesson: a tight team with a shared asset registry beats a large team with a group chat.

## What juries actually reward

Across Runway AIFF, Project Odyssey, Reply AIFF, the Higgsfield Action Contest, and MIT's hackathon, judges reward the same things: **story over spectacle, authorial intent, character/POV discipline, originality, sound design, and polish as the floor.** They punish: visible morphing/inconsistency, generic "AI slop," tech demos without narrative, and AI that pulls focus from the story.

Distilled: narrative cohesion + committed POV + intentional AI use + pro-grade sound + consistent visuals. Originality is the tiebreaker; polish is the entry ticket.

Direct for that jury, story, cinematography, craft, not for the AI-tools audience. The film that wins is the one that would work if nobody knew how it was made.

## Craft patterns from judged work

Higgsfield's Action Contest (8,752 submissions, 139 countries) shows what experienced filmmakers do with AI:

- **"GRANDMA vs WASP"**, Muhannad Nassar & Simon Meyer. The makers never met in person; fully async workflow across time zones. *Lesson:* decentralized production works when the asset pipeline is disciplined.
- **"CUPID"**, Shestak Nikolay, actor/filmmaker. Concepts that would need prohibitive traditional budgets. *Lesson:* AI used for impossible scale, not novelty.
- **"SCRATCH"**, Ash & Aram Gevorkyan, made in **five days**. Director + 3D/VFX generalist duo; production value audiences mistook for a studio trailer. *Lesson:* a tight team with real craft compresses schedules AI can't compress alone.

Pattern: experienced filmmakers using AI for impossible scale, tight teams, real craft, not tech demos.

## Study the open-sourced winners

Higgsfield open-sourced three of its own AI films with every prompt, camera setting, and asset published. Before spending a credit, study a complete production's actual prompts and settings, it's the closest thing to sitting in someone else's edit bay.

## Competition-entry delivery checklist

- [ ] Final cut locked to the runtime cap, master to spec (aspect, watermark/packshot if required)
- [ ] All music/voice/sound rights cleared and documented in the asset registry
- [ ] No uncleared likeness anywhere in the film
- [ ] Credits and key art treated as craft, not an afterthought
- [ ] Team roles and handoffs defined before production starts
