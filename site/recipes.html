Shoot recipes.

The guide's recipe skills as cards: the procedure, the inputs, a worked example, and the pitfalls — for the twelve shots and sequences every AI film needs. {count} recipes

Shoot a chase sequence

Straightforward

Kinetic cutting with geography the audience can follow.

Use when: Chases, pursuits, escapes.

Inputs

  • The geography (map it!)
  • Beats: start / complications / turn / button

Steps

  1. Establish geography in ONE wide before the running starts.
  2. Alternate pursuer/pursued POVs; keep screen direction consistent.
  3. 2–4s shots; camera pace matches subject pace.
  4. End on the button: the caught moment or the clean escape — never a fade.

Worked example

90s chase: 1 geography wide + 24 kinetic shots, screen direction locked left-to-right.

Pitfalls

  • Flipping screen direction — the audience gets lost in one cut.

Shoot a dialogue scene

Intermediate

Coverage that cuts: the complete dialogue-scene recipe.

Use when: Any conversation between two characters.

Inputs

  • Dialogue script
  • Location plate
  • Character registries

Steps

  1. Master: wide two-shot establishing geography (one generation).
  2. Coverage: over-shoulder both directions + clean singles.
  3. Close-ups only at the emotional peaks — ration them.
  4. Match eyelines across every setup; the blocking diagram earns its keep here.
  5. Cut on the dialogue, not the motion; J/L cuts into and out of the scene.

Worked example

8-line scene: 1 wide + 2 OTS + 2 singles + 1 CU peak = 6 generations, ~$15 at 720p.

Pitfalls

  • Generating 20 angles 'for options' — coverage is a plan, not a lottery.

Shoot a dream sequence

Straightforward

Surreal logic with emotional continuity.

Use when: Dreams, memories, altered states.

Inputs

  • The emotion the dream externalizes
  • 2–3 impossible images

Steps

  1. Orbit + atmosphere carry the effect: slow 360s, haze, impossible light.
  2. Logic of emotion, not physics — but keep ONE continuity thread (a color, an object).
  3. Sound design: reversed elements, sub-bass, washed dialogue.
  4. Enter and exit on match cuts — the seam is the art.

Worked example

Grief dream: slow orbit around an empty chair, bioluminescent haze, her voice washed under.

Pitfalls

  • Random surrealism — dreams need one logic, even if it's emotional.

Shoot an establishing sequence

Intermediate

Place, time, tone in under 15 seconds.

Use when: Openings and act transitions.

Inputs

  • Location
  • Time of day
  • The tone word

Steps

  1. Aerial or wide pull-back revealing geography (dawn mist optional but effective).
  2. One detail shot that says 'people live here.'
  3. Time-of-day baked into the plate; tone in the grade.
  4. Under 15 seconds — establishing is a door, not a room.

Worked example

Coastal town, dawn: drone pull-back + fishing boat detail, 12 seconds, cool grade.

Pitfalls

  • The 45-second travelogue opening — judges fast-forward it.

Shoot a fight scene

Intermediate

Readable action: geography, rhythm, impact.

Use when: Fights, brawls, duels.

Inputs

  • The fight's story beat (who wants what)
  • Geography map
  • Impact moments (3–5)

Steps

  1. Wide master of the geography — the audience must know where everyone is.
  2. Build in phrases: 3–4 shots per exchange, impact on the cut.
  3. Vary shot size: wides for geography, tights for impact.
  4. Sound sells hits: whoosh → impact → aftermath, layered.

Worked example

45s duel: 1 master + 18 phrase shots, 4 designed impacts, screen direction locked.

Pitfalls

  • Shaky-cam mush — readable beats hit harder than chaotic ones.

Land the finale button

Straightforward

The last image the jury remembers.

Use when: Endings.

Inputs

  • The film's theme in one image
  • The final sound

Steps

  1. Choose ONE image that contains the whole film — the button.
  2. Hold it longer than feels comfortable.
  3. Resolve the sound: silence, single note, or the theme restated.
  4. No epilogue text explaining what we just felt.

Worked example

Final: the empty chair in morning light, held 6 seconds, room tone only.

Pitfalls

  • Explaining the ending — trust the image.

Shoot a horror short

Straightforward

Dread engineering: what you don't show.

Use when: Horror/suspense competition entries.

Inputs

  • The fear (name it in one sentence)
  • Location plate with practicals

Steps

  1. Slow, hesitant camera verbs; danger hinted, never shown.
  2. Low-key lighting, deep shadows, motivated practicals.
  3. Sound does 70%: whispers, creaks, sub-bass — picture stays restrained.
  4. One reveal, earned late. Everything before it is promise.

Worked example

3-min horror: 18 slow shots, one 4-second reveal at 2:40, sub-bass throughout.

Pitfalls

  • Showing the monster early — the audience's imagination out-renders any model.

Shoot an interview

Intermediate

Credible talking heads: the documentary recipe.

Use when: Testimonials, documentary, UGC.

Inputs

  • Questions/script
  • Location with depth
  • Two-camera plan (even if one is AI)

Steps

  1. MCU, eye-level, locked-off tripod — say 'locked-off' explicitly.
  2. Background with depth and one practical; nothing distracting.
  3. Short answers per generation; edit for rhythm, not completeness.
  4. Cutaways: hands, environment, archival — the interview needs air.

Worked example

5-min interview: 14 answers, 6 cutaways, locked-off MCU throughout.

Pitfalls

  • Wandering framing — interviews live or die on stillness.

Cut a montage

Intermediate

Time compression with emotional direction.

Use when: Training, travel, transformation, passage of time.

Inputs

  • The change the montage must show
  • 5–8 image ideas
  • Music with build

Steps

  1. One idea per shot, 2–3s each — montages are lists with rhythm.
  2. Progress the change visibly: each shot further along than the last.
  3. Match cut or graphic match between ideas for flow.
  4. End on the transformed state, held longer than the rest.

Worked example

Training montage: 8 shots, dawn→dusk light progression, final hold on the champion.

Pitfalls

  • Random pretty shots — every montage shot must advance the change.

Shoot a music video

Intermediate

Beat-mapped visuals with style velocity.

Use when: Music videos and visualizers.

Inputs

  • Track with BPM map
  • Style lock
  • Performance vs abstract ratio

Steps

  1. Map the track: sections, drops, BPM — the edit follows the music.
  2. ~3s shots; a new idea every 8 bars.
  3. Style velocity: 2–3 distinct looks, unified by grade.
  4. Performance anchor: one human (or human-like) thread through the abstraction.

Worked example

3:24 at 118 BPM: 62 shots, 3 looks, dancer thread, cut on every drop.

Pitfalls

  • Ignoring the waveform — cut WITH the music or don't cut at all.

Shoot a product spot

Intermediate

The 30-second product film: macro, orbit, lifestyle.

Use when: Commercials, spec ads, portfolio pieces.

Inputs

  • Product
  • The one feature to fetishize
  • Lifestyle context

Steps

  1. Hero macro: orbit + focus pull on the feature (Wan 2.6 / Seedance).
  2. Texture shots: steam, sheen, fabric — prompted explicitly.
  3. Lifestyle: product in a human hand, in a real-feeling world.
  4. Structure: hook (0–3s macro) → features → lifestyle → packshot.

Worked example

Watch spot: macro orbit + crystal-to-dial focus pull → wrist lifestyle → logo packshot.

Pitfalls

  • Feature soup — one feature per spot, fetishized.

Cut a trailer

Straightforward

The 60–90s competition weapon, structured.

Use when: Trailers and teasers.

Inputs

  • Three biggest moments
  • Music with dynamics

Steps

  1. See workflow-trailer-structure; this is its production recipe.
  2. Pull selects from the film's hero shots — trailers are made from winners.
  3. Sound-design the trailer separately: it's its own film.
  4. Title card on the button image; laurel space on the end card.

Worked example

75s trailer from a 4-min short: 22 hero shots re-cut, new sound design, title smash.

Pitfalls

  • Recutting the whole film shorter — a trailer is built, not trimmed.
