Rights, Disclosure & Legal

Not legal advice. What follows is documented policy and law as researched on 2026-10-11, with confidence flags. Rules change fast and vary by jurisdiction — consult an entertainment attorney for your production. When a claim below is single-source, it's flagged; verify against primary sources before acting on it.

The two layers: platform policy and law

Two separate layers apply simultaneously. Platform policy costs you distribution or your account. Law costs you money. Neither asks whether you used AI — both ask whether the result looks real.

YouTube detail: disclosure is required for synthetic/cloned voices of real people, digitally altered footage making someone appear to say or do something they didn't, and fabricated depictions of real events. Not required: unrealistic/fantastical content, animation, production assistance (script help, thumbnails, color correction), cloning your own voice. For sensitive topics (elections, health, major news) the label can appear on the video itself. YouTube states disclosure does not by itself limit reach or monetization.

AI disclosure: awards and festivals

  • 99th Academy Awards (rules approved May 2026): AI tools "neither help nor harm" a nomination — but acting nominations require performances demonstrably performed by humans with consent (credited in legal billing), and screenplay categories require human authorship. (Reuters)
  • Cannes 2026: films primarily driven by generative AI are excluded from Palme d'Or competition — not an outright ban; AI in production/post-production is increasingly accepted, and a separate AI film festival ran alongside. (Reuters, May 2026)
  • Human Provenance in Film standard (launched at the Cannes market, May 2026): three voluntary tiers — No AI Used / Assistive AI / Generative AI. (Single-source)
  • Audience pressure is real: 72% of US viewers surveyed want AI use disclosed every time. (Single-source survey claim — treat as directional.)

AI disclosure: laws with teeth

  • EU AI Act, Article 50: legal transparency duty for anyone publishing AI-generated images, audio, or video that could pass for real. Deployer transparency obligations took effect August 2026. Applies regardless of platform policy. (Single-source summary — verify against the official EU text.)
  • New York General Business Law § 396-b(3): advertisements containing a synthetic performer (with actual knowledge) must conspicuously disclose it. Penalties: $1,000 first violation, $5,000 each subsequent. Signed December 2025, championed by SAG-AFTRA. (Penalty figures single-source — confirm with counsel.)
  • TAKE IT DOWN Act (federal, signed 2025): notice-and-removal in effect May 2026 — platforms must remove non-consensual intimate AI imagery within 48 hours of a valid request, plus reasonable efforts against identical copies.
  • NO FAKES Act (federal, proposed — not law): would create a federal right against non-consensual digital replicas. Do not present as law.

Likeness & rights: the rules

The template: licensed likeness. The Cully Hill Boys licensed digital likenesses of four performers (Israel Adesanya, Quinton "Rampage" Jackson, streamer N3on, Matt "MK" Kiatipis). Each signed commercial licensing agreements, supplied high-resolution photos, and recorded ~2 minutes of audio; models mapped expressions, kinematics, and vocal patterns. Biometric data deleted within 30 days. Each performer signed off on the script. The screenplay was licensed from The Black List with generative-AI rights separated from traditional-adaptation rights.

The legal landscape (US):

  • No general federal right of publicity exists. Protection depends on state law, and most state statutes predate generative AI.
  • Tennessee ELVIS Act (effective July 1, 2024): first state law targeting AI voice clones — protects voice simulations, imposes liability for knowingly distributing unauthorized voice/likeness uses, and reaches tool providers whose "primary purpose" is replicating a person's voice or image.
  • States with explicit AI/digital-replica provisions: California (digital-replica contract provisions; deceased personality rights), New York, Illinois, Arkansas, Hawaii, Washington.
  • Deceased persons: rights are controlled by estates — the 2026 Val Kilmer synthetic performance was made with family consent and estate compensation.

Union guardrails (union productions only, but they set the norms): SAG-AFTRA's 2026 deal strengthened consent and compensation with new synthetics guardrails — a principle strongly favoring human performances, digital replicas protected from crossing picket lines, protections for minors, and an articulable business reason required to scan someone. The unions' shared rule: software isn't a writer or performer — AI material must be disclosed and can't take credits or replace people.

Practical read: never generate a recognizable real person — face or voice — without a signed likeness license. Voice is separately protected (Tennessee). Deceased persons require estate clearance. Keep the license, the input-materials log, and the data-deletion record together.

Music & audio rights: the core trap

A commercial license is not copyright. Yes, you can use AI-generated music from a paid Suno/Udio account in a commercial film — but you will not own the copyright to it. The US Copyright Office position: no copyright without sufficient human authorship. A platform's commercial license gives you a contractual right to use the output; it cannot grant the bundle of exclusive rights (reproduction, distribution, derivatives, public performance) if no copyright vests.

This gap is what stops distributors, insurers, and festivals: their paperwork asks for proof of rights / chain of title, and an AI track produces a thin file.

For maximum defensibility: compose with human-arranged tools (AIVA Pro), license royalty-free libraries (Soundraw), or commission humans. Document everything in the music cue sheet.

The paperwork stack

Standard indie deliverables (Tribeca Film Institute Distribution 101), plus AI-specific records — no standard form exists, so build the folder yourself:

Standard stack:

  • Chain of title: script rights, contributor agreements, location releases, music licenses + cue sheet, archival/trademark clearances, distribution agreements. Missing documents can block broadcast, streaming, or sale.
  • E&O (errors & omissions) insurance: required by most distributors before licensing; covers defamation, copyright infringement, invasion of privacy. Maintain ~3 years post-release. AI wrinkle: purely AI-generated material may not qualify for copyright protection — confirm with your broker that coverage maps to your actual rights.
  • Technical: DCP, ProRes masters, M&E track, captions/subtitles, key art, trailer, press kit.

AI-specific additions:

  1. Likeness licenses for any real-person face/voice reference (written license, defined inputs, talent approval, data-deletion clause). No license = do not generate the person.
  2. Platform subscription records proving commercial-tier status during generation + download/generation logs.
  3. Music license file + cue sheet — every track's source, tier, generation date, and the platform's rights grant.
  4. AI disclosure log: what was disclosed, where, when (YouTube toggle, TikTok label, Meta tool).
  5. No union performer releases needed for synthetic "performers" — they're not performers. What you need instead is (1) above.
  6. Bring an entertainment attorney in early — "better now than trying to retrofit your project after the fact."

Rights checklist

  • Every real-person face/voice has a signed likeness license (or is not in the film)
  • All AI music generated on a paid commercial tier, receipts + cue sheet filed
  • Disclosure toggles set correctly on every platform at upload
  • Chain of title complete; E&O bound (coverage confirmed for AI-generated material)
  • AI disclosure log maintained (what, where, when)
  • Attorney reviewed before distributor conversations
