Learn craft, not buttons.

Every tool below will be renamed, repriced, or replaced within a year. The decisions underneath them have been stable for a century. This page maps the craft to the controls, so when the buttons move, you don't have to relearn filmmaking.

The thesis

A tool teaches you where to click. Craft teaches you what to decide. The click expires; the decision compounds. Every section below names the craft decision first, the thing a director has always had to get right, then shows where that decision lives in today's major tools. Control names change monthly; the guide's platform field guide tracks the current specifics. What follows is the stable layer.

This is decision guidance, not shopping advice. There are no rankings here and no "best tool", the model quiz scores tools against your actual job, and the when-to-use-what chapter covers trade-offs honestly.

1. Text-to-video: the shot

The craft decision: one sentence that says what the shot is, the verb, the subject, the frame. "A tired detective crosses a rain-lit parking lot, seen from a low tracking shot" is a decision. A paragraph of adjectives is decoration. The model needs the blocking, the lens behavior, and what to exclude; it does not need your mood board described in prose.

2. Image-to-video: the plate

The craft decision: the still frame is the film. Composition, light, wardrobe, production design, and lens character are decided in the image, the video model only adds motion. ONEIRIC's consistent-lens-look trick is the proof: bake the optics into the plate and the video inherits them; ask for them in the video prompt and they drift shot to shot. The plate itself becomes the lens.

3. Upscaling: the finish

The craft decision: resolution is a delivery spec, not a creative choice. Generate drafts at 720p, upscale only the keepers. Upscaling cannot rescue bad blocking, dead eyes, or broken continuity, it sharpens them. The decision is which shots deserve the spend, made at the rough cut, not in the generator.

4. Voice and dialogue: the performance

The craft decision: who is speaking, and what are they doing with the words, the tactic, not the emotion label. The voice is cast like an actor (register, timbre, tempo, manner, the Voice Bible), directed like an actor (pace, breath, emphasis), and locked like an asset (pasted verbatim into every generation). The long-form chapter covers the three architectures: native audio generated with the shot, human-recorded then AI-converted, or decoupled TTS married in the edit.

5. Editing and assembly: the film

The craft decision: what the audience feels, in what order. AI didn't change editing, every documented long-form production cuts in Resolve or Premiere, with a scheduled generation-supervision pass after the rough cut and a colorist whose job is unification (every generation ships its own grade). The AI-specific decisions are where to cut (on the dialogue, not the motion, J/L cuts carry scenes across generations better than matched action) and when to stop generating (picture lock means no new generations without notice to color and sound).

How to choose, then

Pick tools by the job, not the brand: the quiz scores all eighteen models against your use case, budget, and audio needs from data. Then hold them to the craft standard on this page, if a tool can't serve the decision in the left column, the tool is wrong for the shot, whatever its marketing says. When a new model launches next month, you won't need a new tutorial. You'll need this page.
